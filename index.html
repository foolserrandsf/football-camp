Station 1 · 25 min

Personnel: who's on the field

Every offense has 11 players. Six are fixed: five offensive linemen who only block, plus the quarterback. The other five are chosen from three types: running backs (RB, carry the ball), a fullback (FB, a bigger back who blocks for the RB), tight ends (TE, line up beside a tackle, half blocker, half receiver) and wide receivers (WR, split out to catch passes).

The code: first digit = backs (RB + FB), second = tight ends. Receivers are whatever's left of 5.

11 = 1 back, 1 TE, 3 WR. The NFL default.

21 = 2 backs (FB + RB), 1 TE, 2 WR. Built to run.

CodeBacks / TE / WRWhat it tells you
111 / 1 / 3Balanced, leans pass.
121 / 2 / 2Extra blocker: run, or play-action off the run look.
212 / 1 / 2Fullback in: downhill running.
101 / 0 / 4Spread out: pass or fast tempo. Common in college.
13 / 22jumboShort yardage and goal line.

The defense answers by counting DBs

The defense can see who jogs on. It swaps players to match: more linebackers for run-heavy groups, more defensive backs (DBs) for pass-heavy ones.

Base: 3 LB, 4 DBs. Against 21 or 12.

Nickel: 2 LB, 5 DBs. Against 11: the nickelback (NB) covers the slot receiver.

Dime: 1 LB, 6 DBs. Obvious passing downs.

Connecting it: a mismatch is the tell. Heavy offense vs a light nickel defense favors the run. 11 personnel vs base leaves a slow linebacker covering a slot receiver, which favors the pass.

Station 2 · 25 min

The box: count and compare

The "box" is the area near the line, from one end of the offensive line to the other, about 5–7 yards deep. It's where runs are stopped. Two counts, before every snap:

  • Blockers: 5 linemen + any tight ends + a fullback. The QB and RB don't count.
  • Box defenders: every defender standing inside that area.

Six blockers vs six in the box. Every defender can be blocked, so the run has a fair chance.

Seven blockers vs eight in the box. One defender will be unblocked, so the defense is daring the offense to throw.

The rule

Box defenders ≤ blockers: the run has numbers. Box defenders > blockers: expect a pass, often play-action (Station 5), because the defenders crowding the line have left fewer players in coverage.

Station 3 · 35 min

How the defense reads you back

It never knows the play. It reacts on a schedule.

The defense sees the offense's personnel and substitutes to match (Station 1). The coach radios a call chosen by down, distance and field position. When the offense lines up, defenders align by rules ("our run stopper lines up over the tight end"). After the snap, each defender watches one "key" player, usually a lineman: lineman fires forward = run, steps back = pass.

Expecting a run (3rd & 1): heavy box.

Expecting a pass (3rd & 10): dime, and rushers ignore the run.

Coverages: count the deep defenders

The number in "Cover 2" or "Cover 3" is how many defenders split up the deep part of the field, not how many receivers are covered. You can see the deep defenders before the snap, so counting them tells you the coverage.

Cover 2: two safeties each take a deep half; five defenders cover short areas. Soft spot: deep middle, between the safeties.

Cover 3: two corners and a safety each take a deep third; four underneath. The other safety joins the run defense. Soft spot: where two thirds meet.

Cover 4: four deep quarters, three underneath. Hard to throw deep on. Soft spot: short throws to the sideline.

Cover 1: man coverage (dashed ties) on every receiver, one safety deep as backup.

Cover 0: man coverage, nobody deep, everyone else blitzes.

2Cover 2 or Cover 4Run the ball; short and middle throws
1Cover 1 or Cover 3Play-action and deep shots
0Cover 0Throw in under 2 seconds

How zone defenders keep track

Each zone has edges set by field markings. When a receiver crosses from one zone into the next, the first defender calls it out and hands him off.

  • Zones have limits: a depth (short zones about 10–12 yards) and a width marked by the hash marks and painted numbers.
  • Eyes on the QB: most zone defenders watch the QB and sense receivers entering their area.
  • Nobody in my zone: drift toward the nearest receiver or where the QB is looking.
  • Match coverage: many modern zones become man once routes declare; each defender locks onto whoever is in his area.

The offense beats zone by "flooding" one area: two receivers, one defender.

Motion tells man from zone

Defenses disguise, showing two deep safeties and rotating one down at the snap. You'll be wrong sometimes. The read is still right far more often than guessing.

Station 4 · 30 min

Formations: where they line up

Personnel is who. Formation is where. Two things define it: where the QB stands, and how the receivers are spread. The tighter everyone is packed, the more the offense wants to run; the more spread out, the more it wants to pass.

T formation: three backs in a row. The run can go either way and the defense can’t tell who gets the ball. Today it’s a goal-line look ("full house").

I-formation: FB leads, RB follows. Power running and play-action.

Singleback: one back, QB under center. Balanced.

Shotgun: QB starts back, sees the defense, no drop needed. Leans pass.

Pistol: short shotgun with the RB directly behind. Runs either direction; QB can run too.

Trips (3x1): three receivers to one side pull coverage over, often isolating the lone receiver on the other side.

Empty: no back to block, so it’s nearly always a quick pass.

Bunch: three receivers in a tight triangle.

Why bunch beats man coverage

In man coverage, each defender must chase his own receiver. When three receivers start inches apart and cross right after the snap, the chasers have to run through the same small space. They bump into each other or get caught behind a receiver, and their man gets a step of separation. That's a "rub." It's legal as long as the receiver is running his route, not deliberately blocking.

Against zone, it barely works, because nobody chases: defenders stay on their side and take whoever arrives. Man defenses copy this with a "switch" call.

Station 5 · 40 min

The seven plays that matter

Every play exists because a defense overcommitted to stop something else. For each: what it looks like and what it beats.

Inside zone. Beats light boxes and linebackers who guess.

Outside zone. Beats slow defensive fronts. The run look many NFL offenses fake on play-action.

Power / counter. Spot it by the pulling lineman. Beats short yardage and defenses that flow too fast.

Quick game. Beats blitzes and corners playing far off their receivers.

Play-action. Beats loaded boxes: the more defenders crowd the line for the run, the emptier it is behind them.

Screen. Beats heavy blitzes and fast pass rushers by letting them run past the play.

RPO (run-pass option). Beats any defender trying to stop the run and the pass at once: whatever he does is wrong.

Connecting it

Loaded box (Station 2) → play-action. Blitz (Cover 0) → screen or quick game. Two deep safeties → run. Once you've counted the box and the safeties, ask "what is this defense giving up?" That's usually the play.

Your playbook

The clock: why running "keeps the other QB off the field"

The game clock keeps running after a run that ends in bounds and stops after an incomplete pass. Only one offense is on the field at a time, so every minute you hold the ball is a minute the other quarterback sits.

Game script: where this meets your brief

Teams that trail pass more, stop the clock, and run more plays. Teams that lead run the ball to burn clock. That's why a blowout lifts the trailing team's receiving yards and the leading team's RB carries, and why a fast early lead can drag a total lower in the second half.

Field position: where a drive starts decides how far it has to go.

Station 8 · 40 min

The drill: predict one quarter

Put on any game replay. Pause before every snap. Log the game clock and yard line from the TV graphic, what you see, and your call, then play it and log what happened. The clock and yard line let your game review find the exact play, even if you skip snaps. For a bonus, name the exact play you expect (Station 5); the review grades it and corrects anything you logged wrong. Do 15–20 snaps, about one quarter.

The benchmark

Roughly 55–60% of NFL plays are passes, so guessing "pass" every time scores about that. Beat 70% and your reads are doing real work. Snaps save to GitHub, by game and quarter, in the same place as the standalone Film Room app, so once you set up sync they’re on every device you use, and each game gets an automatic review the morning after.
